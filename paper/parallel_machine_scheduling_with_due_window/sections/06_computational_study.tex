\section{Computational Study}\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the computational performance of the proposed exact solution
framework and the sensitivity of the outsourcing decisions to key parameters. We
first disable outsourcing and compare the proposed continuous-time
NG-DSSR pricing algorithm with the time-indexed pricing framework of
\citet{bulhoes2020exact} for the resulting pure parallel-machine scheduling problem.
We next compare the two outsourcing formulations. Finally, we conduct sensitivity
analyses of the number of job families, outsourcing availability and baseline price,
and quantity discounts. All algorithms were implemented in Java and use IBM ILOG
CPLEX 22.1.1 as the LP solver. Each run uses a single thread and is subject to a time
limit of 10,800 seconds.

\subsection{Instance Generation}\label{sec:instance-generation}

We derive the test instances from the single-machine total weighted
earliness--tardiness instances used by \citet{tanaka2009exact} and distributed in
Tanaka's online repository at
\url{https://sites.google.com/site/shunjitanaka/sips/benchmark-results-sips}.
The instances generated for this study are available at
\textit{[data repository URL]}. Consider a benchmark instance with job set
$\mathcal J^0$. For each job $j\in\mathcal J^0$, the benchmark provides a
processing time $p_j$, a due date $d_j^0$, and earliness and tardiness weights
$\alpha_j$ and $\beta_j$. From each selected benchmark instance, we either retain
all jobs or extract a subset $\mathcal J\subseteq\mathcal J^0$ to form a base job
set; hence, $\mathcal J=\mathcal J^0$ when the benchmark instance is used in full.
We construct five fixed base job sets for each
$n=|\mathcal J|\in\{20,40,50,60,80,100\}$, giving 30 base job sets in total.

For each base job set $\mathcal J$, we construct three parallel-machine variants,
with $m\in\{2,3,4\}$ for $n\leq 60$ and $m\in\{3,4,5\}$ otherwise. For each job
$j\in\mathcal J$, the due window is defined as
\[
[d_j^e,d_j^l]=[\max\{0,c_j-W_j\},c_j+W_j],
\]
where $c_j$ is the due-window center and $W_j$ is its half-width. We derive the
center from the benchmark due date $d_j^0$ in two steps. First, we define the
retained workload ratio
\[
r=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal J}p_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal J^0}p_i},
\]
which equals one when $\mathcal J=\mathcal J^0$ and rescales the benchmark due
date when only a subset of jobs is retained. Second, we set
$m^{\mathrm{ref}}=3$ for $n\leq60$ and $m^{\mathrm{ref}}=4$ otherwise, and compute
\[
c_j=\left\lceil\frac{\operatorname{round}(d_j^0r)}{m^{\mathrm{ref}}}\right\rceil,
\]
where division by $m^{\mathrm{ref}}$ converts the due date from the single-machine
benchmark horizon to the parallel-machine horizon. The same reference value is used
for all three actual machine levels of a base job set, so varying $m$ changes
internal capacity without simultaneously changing the due windows. Finally, we set
$W_j=0$ in the zero-width class and independently draw $W_j$ from
$\{80,\ldots,120\}$ and $\{280,\ldots,320\}$ in the narrow and wide classes,
respectively.

For every job set, we generate two matched sequence-dependent setup structures. In
the random instances, setup times contain no explicit job grouping and are converted
into a directed metric. The corresponding family instances are built from the same
base metric, partition the jobs into three to six approximately balanced families,
and add a changeover component to between-family transitions. This construction
reflects the
smaller within-family and larger between-family setups commonly considered in family
scheduling \citep{schutten1996parallel,schaller2000flowline}, while keeping the
overall setup intensity comparable across the two structures. In both cases, the
average setup time is approximately $0.5\bar p$, where $\bar p$ is the average
processing time, no setup time exceeds $\bar p$, and the directed triangle
inequality is satisfied. The average between-family setup in the family instances is
approximately four to five times the average within-family setup. The resulting
setup intensity lies between the small and large setup classes used by
\citet{bulhoes2020exact}. Setup costs are defined as $\kappa_{ij}=20s_{ij}$.

Each original-scale production environment is completed by an outsourcing tariff.
Following \citet{lee2011twostage}, who scale outsourcing costs with processing
requirements, we define the standard quotation of job $j$ as
\[
q_j=p_j\max\{\alpha_j,\beta_j\}.
\]
Thus, the quotation increases with both the processing requirement and the larger
of the two unit timing penalties. We create low, medium, and high outsourcing price
levels by setting the undiscounted baseline cost to
$b_j(\lambda)=\lambda q_j$, where $\lambda\in\{0.5,1,2\}$. Unless stated otherwise,
the payment function applies a continuous incremental quantity discount, consistent
with the tariff structures studied in production outsourcing and private-fleet
routing \citep{lu2021discount,dabia2019rich}. To apply the same absolute tariff to
all instance sizes, the breakpoints $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are fixed at 25\% and 50\% of
the median total standard quotation among the 50-job sets. The marginal rates over
the three intervals are $1$, $0.85$, and $0.70$, with the intercepts chosen to keep
the payment function continuous. Both breakpoints and marginal rates remain fixed
across all instances.

Combining the 30 base job sets with three machine levels, two setup structures, and
three due-window classes yields 540 original-scale production environments. The
three outsourcing price levels expand this set to 1,620 complete instances. The
formulation comparison and sensitivity analyses use these complete instances,
whereas the internal-pricing comparison uses the same production data with
outsourcing disabled.

\subsection{Comparison of Internal Pricing Methods}\label{sec:pricing-comparison}

The first experiment considers the pure parallel-machine scheduling problem, in which
all jobs must be processed internally. We compare the proposed continuous-time
NG-DSSR pricing algorithm with the time-indexed pricing framework of
\citet{bulhoes2020exact}, using the latter both without and with rank-1 cuts. The
comparison is designed to identify the conditions under which the continuous-time and
time-indexed approaches are most effective, rather than to evaluate either method at
a single time resolution.

To assess sensitivity to the magnitude of the time parameters, we augment the
original-scale instances with medium- and high-scale variants. For each job, its
processing time and due-window center are independently multiplied by integers drawn
from $\{5,\ldots,15\}$ in the medium-scale class and $\{15,\ldots,25\}$ in the
high-scale class. Each setup matrix is scaled by the resulting change in total
processing workload, while the narrow and wide half-width ranges are multiplied by
10 and 20, respectively. The unit earliness and tardiness penalties remain unchanged.
The three time scales produce 1,620 scheduling instances and 4,860 algorithm runs.

Performance is examined across job sizes, machine levels, due-window classes, setup
structures, and time scales. In particular, the matched random and family instances
have comparable average setup intensity but differ in how short and long setup arcs
are organized. Their comparison therefore isolates whether family structure, rather
than a general increase in setup times, changes the relative behavior of continuous-
time and time-indexed pricing.

\subsection{Comparison of Outsourcing Formulations}\label{sec:outsourcing-formulation-comparison}

The next experiment evaluates how the representation of outsourcing affects exact
solution performance. We compare formulations \textup{(SP1)} and \textup{(SP2)} on
the 1,620 complete instances defined in Section~\ref{sec:instance-generation}. Both
formulations use NG-DSSR for internal pricing and start from the same initial solution
for each instance. The experimental difference is confined to the outsourcing
representation: \textup{(SP1)} generates outsourced job sets as columns, whereas
\textup{(SP2)} represents the outsourcing decisions explicitly in the restricted
master problem. The comparison comprises 3,240 runs and spans all job sizes, machine
levels, due-window classes, setup structures, and outsourcing price levels. Results
are also separated by random and family setups to determine whether the relative
performance of the two formulations is robust to the structure of the internal
changeovers.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}\label{sec:sensitivity-analysis}

We first examine how the number of job families relative to the number of machines
affects NG-DSSR and time-indexed pricing. Using the formulation selected from the
preceding comparison, we then analyze the effects of outsourcing availability and
baseline price, followed by the effect of quantity discounts. All comparisons are
paired so that the factors not under examination remain unchanged.

\subsubsection{Impact of Family Structure on Internal Pricing}
\label{sec:family-pricing-impact}

The matched random--family comparison identifies whether setup organization affects
the two pricing approaches, but it does not by itself explain how this effect depends
on the relationship between the number of families and the number of machines. We
therefore conduct a focused experiment with $n=40$, $m=3$, the original time scale,
zero-width due windows, and no outsourcing. For each of the five base job sets, we
construct matched instances with $F\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ approximately balanced families,
while holding the job data, average setup intensity, setup-cost coefficient, and all
other generation rules fixed. This yields 25 instances that differ only in
their family partition and the resulting organization of setup times.

The experiment tests whether pricing behavior changes when the number of families
first exceeds the number of machines. In an elementary schedule, $F>m$ requires at
least one machine to process jobs from multiple families and incur a between-family
changeover. A non-elementary time-indexed relaxation may partly avoid this requirement
through repeated job visits, whereas NG-DSSR must progressively eliminate such
repetitions to certify elementary pricing. We therefore use changes in the root
relaxation and repeated-job structure to interpret the time-indexed results, and use
the number of DSSR rounds and exact-pricing effort as complementary evidence for
NG-DSSR. A threshold interpretation is made only if the change around $F=m+1$ is
consistently accompanied by the corresponding change in repeated-job behavior across
the five matched job sets.

\subsubsection{Impact of Outsourcing}\label{sec:impact-outsourcing}

This analysis assesses the value of making outsourcing available and the sensitivity
of the outsourcing decisions to its baseline price. For each original-scale
production environment, the solution without outsourcing is compared with the
solutions under the low, medium, and high price levels. These paired comparisons show
how internal capacity, due-window width, and setup structure shape outsourcing usage
and the resulting cost composition. The random--family comparison further indicates
whether outsourcing primarily relieves timing congestion or also reorganizes the
internal schedule by avoiding costly between-family changeovers. Accordingly, the
analysis distinguishes direct outsourcing payments from the associated changes in
internal earliness, tardiness, and setup costs.

\subsubsection{Impact of Quantity Discounts}\label{sec:impact-discounts}

Following \citet{dabia2019rich}, this analysis examines whether a quantity discount
only lowers the payment for jobs that would already be outsourced or also changes the
outsourcing decision itself. We use the five 50-job sets at the original time scale
and the medium outsourcing price. Across all combinations of three machine levels,
three due-window classes, and two setup structures, the default marginal rates
$(1,0.85,0.70)$ are compared with a tariff that applies the undiscounted rate
throughout. This produces 90 paired production environments and isolates the discount
effect while holding the standard job quotations and production conditions fixed.
Comparing the random and family instances additionally tests whether discounts are
more likely to alter the outsourced job set when outsourcing a group of jobs can also
simplify the remaining family-changeover structure, rather than merely reduce the
payment for an otherwise unchanged outsourcing decision.
