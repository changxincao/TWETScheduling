\section{Computational Study}\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the computational performance of the proposed exact solution
framework and the sensitivity of the outsourcing decisions to key parameters. The first
experiment disables outsourcing and benchmarks the proposed continuous-time NG-DSSR
pricing algorithm against the time-indexed pricing framework of
\citet{bulhoes2020exact} for the resulting pure parallel-machine scheduling problem.
We then compare the computational performance of the two outsourcing formulations.
Finally, we examine the effects of outsourcing availability, the baseline
outsourcing price, and quantity discounts. All algorithms were implemented in Java
and use IBM ILOG CPLEX 22.1.1 as the LP solver. Each run uses a single thread and is
subject to a time limit of 10,800 seconds.

\subsection{Instance Generation}\label{sec:instance-generation}

We derive the test instances from the single-machine total weighted
earliness--tardiness instances used by \citet{tanaka2009exact} and distributed in
Tanaka's online repository at
\url{https://sites.google.com/site/shunjitanaka/sips/benchmark-results-sips}.
The instances generated for this study are available at
\textit{[data repository URL]}. The source files specify a processing time, a due
date, and earliness and tardiness weights for each job. By retaining or extracting
jobs from the source instances, we construct five fixed job sets for each
$n\in\{20,40,50,60,80,100\}$, giving 30 base job sets in total.

For each base job set, we construct three parallel-machine variants, with
$m\in\{2,3,4\}$ for $n\leq 60$ and $m\in\{3,4,5\}$ otherwise. Let $\mathcal J$
denote the jobs in the resulting base job set and $\mathcal J^0$ all jobs in
the Tanaka source instance from which it is constructed. Thus,
$\mathcal J\subseteq\mathcal J^0$, with equality when the source instance is
retained in full. For each job $j\in\mathcal J$, the due window is defined as
\[
[d_j^e,d_j^l]=[\max\{0,c_j-W_j\},c_j+W_j],
\]
where $c_j$ is the due-window center and $W_j$ is its half-width. To construct
$c_j$, let $d_j^0$ denote the due date of job $j$ in the Tanaka source instance
and define the retained workload ratio as
\[
r=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal J}p_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal J^0}p_i},
\]
so that $r=1$ when the complete source instance is retained and $r<1$ when only
a subset of its jobs is used. The due-window center is then computed as
\[
c_j=\left\lceil\frac{\operatorname{round}(d_j^0r)}{m^{\mathrm{ref}}}\right\rceil,
\]
where $m^{\mathrm{ref}}=3$ for $n\leq60$ and $m^{\mathrm{ref}}=4$ otherwise.
Multiplying $d_j^0$ by $r$ adjusts the source single-machine time horizon to the
workload retained in the current job set, while division by $m^{\mathrm{ref}}$
maps that horizon to the parallel-machine setting. The same reference value is
used for all three actual machine levels of a base job set, so varying $m$ changes
internal capacity without simultaneously changing the due windows. Finally, we set
$W_j=0$ in the zero-width class and independently draw $W_j$ from
$\{80,\ldots,120\}$ and $\{280,\ldots,320\}$ in the narrow and wide classes,
respectively.

For every job set, we generate two matched sequence-dependent setup structures. In
the random instances, setup times contain no explicit job grouping and are converted
into a directed metric. The corresponding family instances are built from the same
base metric, partition the jobs into three to six approximately balanced families,
and add a changeover component to between-family transitions. This construction
reflects the
smaller within-family and larger between-family setups commonly considered in family
scheduling \citep{schutten1996parallel,schaller2000flowline}, while keeping the
overall setup intensity comparable across the two structures. In both cases, the
average setup time is approximately $0.5\bar p$, where $\bar p$ is the average
processing time, no setup time exceeds $\bar p$, and the directed triangle
inequality is satisfied. The average between-family setup in the family instances is
approximately four to five times the average within-family setup. The resulting
setup intensity lies between the small and large setup classes used by
\citet{bulhoes2020exact}. Setup costs are defined as $\kappa_{ij}=20s_{ij}$.

Each original-scale production environment is completed by an outsourcing tariff.
Following \citet{lee2011twostage}, who scale outsourcing costs with processing
requirements, we define the standard quotation of job $j$ as
\[
q_j=p_j\max\{\alpha_j,\beta_j\}.
\]
Thus, the quotation increases with both the processing requirement and the larger
of the two unit timing penalties. We create low, medium, and high outsourcing price
levels by setting the undiscounted baseline cost to
$b_j(\lambda)=\lambda q_j$, where $\lambda\in\{0.5,1,2\}$. Unless stated otherwise,
the payment function applies a continuous incremental quantity discount, consistent
with the tariff structures studied in production outsourcing and private-fleet
routing \citep{lu2021discount,dabia2019rich}. To apply the same absolute tariff to
all instance sizes, the breakpoints $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are fixed at 25\% and 50\% of
the median total standard quotation among the 50-job sets. The marginal rates over
the three intervals are $1$, $0.85$, and $0.70$, with the intercepts chosen to keep
the payment function continuous. Both breakpoints and marginal rates remain fixed
across all instances.

Combining the 30 base job sets with three machine levels, two setup structures, and
three due-window classes yields 540 original-scale production environments. The
three outsourcing price levels expand this set to 1,620 complete instances. The
formulation comparison and sensitivity analyses use these complete instances,
whereas the internal-pricing comparison uses the same production data with
outsourcing disabled.

\subsection{Comparison of Internal Pricing Methods}\label{sec:pricing-comparison}

The first experiment considers the pure parallel-machine scheduling problem in which
every job must be processed internally. It compares the proposed continuous-time
NG-DSSR pricing algorithm with the time-indexed pricing method of
\citet{bulhoes2020exact}, implemented both without and with rank-1 cuts. To examine
their sensitivity to the magnitude of the time parameters, we augment the
original-scale instances with medium- and high-scale variants. For each job, its
processing time and due-window center are independently multiplied by integers drawn
from $\{5,\ldots,15\}$ in the medium-scale class and $\{15,\ldots,25\}$ in the
high-scale class. Each setup matrix is scaled by the resulting change in total
processing workload, while the narrow and wide half-width ranges are multiplied by
10 and 20, respectively. The unit earliness and tardiness penalties remain
unchanged. The three time scales produce 1,620 scheduling instances and 4,860
algorithm runs. This design compares the relative performance and applicable ranges
of continuous-time and time-indexed pricing across job sizes, machine levels,
due-window widths, setup structures, and time scales.

\subsection{Comparison of Outsourcing Formulations}\label{sec:outsourcing-formulation-comparison}

The second experiment examines how the representation of outsourcing affects exact
solution performance. We compare formulations \textup{(SP1)} and \textup{(SP2)} on
the 1,620 complete instances defined in Section~\ref{sec:instance-generation}. Both
formulations use NG-DSSR for internal pricing and start from the same initial
solution for each instance. The only experimental difference is the representation
of outsourcing: \textup{(SP1)} generates outsourced job sets as columns, whereas
\textup{(SP2)} represents the outsourcing decisions explicitly in the restricted
master problem. The comparison comprises 3,240 runs and covers all job sizes,
machine levels, due-window classes, setup structures, and outsourcing price levels.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}\label{sec:sensitivity-analysis}

Using the formulation selected from the preceding comparison, we analyze the
sensitivity of the optimal outsourcing decisions to the economic conditions. The
first analysis varies the availability and baseline price of outsourcing, whereas
the second isolates the effect of the quantity-discount schedule. Paired instances
are used so that the production environment remains unchanged within each
comparison.

\subsubsection{Impact of Outsourcing}\label{sec:impact-outsourcing}

This analysis assesses the value of making outsourcing available and the sensitivity
of the outsourcing decisions to its baseline price. For each original-scale
production environment, the solution without outsourcing is compared with the
solutions under the low, medium, and high price levels. The paired comparison reveals
how machine availability, due-window width, and setup structure influence outsourcing
usage and the resulting cost composition. In particular, it distinguishes direct
outsourcing payments from changes in internal timing penalties and setup costs after
jobs that are costly to accommodate internally are assigned to the external
provider.

\subsubsection{Impact of Quantity Discounts}\label{sec:impact-discounts}

Following \citet{dabia2019rich}, this analysis examines whether a quantity discount
only lowers the payment for jobs that would already be outsourced or also changes
the outsourcing decision itself. We use the five 50-job sets at the original time
scale and the medium outsourcing price. Across all combinations of three machine
levels, three due-window classes, and two setup structures, the default marginal
rates $(1,0.85,0.70)$ are compared with a tariff that applies the undiscounted rate
throughout. This produces 90 paired production environments and isolates the effect
of the discount schedule while holding the standard job quotations and production
conditions fixed. The comparison therefore identifies whether the discount changes
only outsourcing expenditure or also the outsourced job set and the associated
internal scheduling costs.
